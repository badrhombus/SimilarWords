# Commonly confused word pairs

***

## Accept vs. Except

Accept is a verb meaning to receive or agree to something; Except is usually a preposition (occasionally a conjunction) introducing an exclusion.[^10][^12]

- **Accept**: To receive or agree to.
- The principal accepted the boy’s story about the broken window.
- **Except**: Aside from or excluding.
- I want all of the mugs to be washed except for the blue one.


## Advice vs. Advise

Advice is a noun meaning guidance or recommendation; Advise is a verb meaning to recommend.[^12]

- **Advice**: A recommendation.
- She gave me excellent advice.
- **Advise**: To recommend.
- The doctor advised him to exercise.


## Affect vs. Effect

Affect is usually a verb meaning to influence; Effect is usually a noun meaning a result or outcome; effect can occasionally be a verb meaning “to bring about”.[^8][^12]

- **Affect**: To influence.
- The cold weather affected attendance.
- **Effect**: A result.
- The medicine had an effect on her symptoms.


## Aisle vs. Isle

Aisle is a noun meaning a walkway between rows; Isle is a noun meaning an island.[^12]

- **Aisle**: A walkway.
- She walked down the aisle at her wedding.
- **Isle**: An island.
- The ferry docked at the isle.


## Allusion vs. Illusion

Allusion is a noun meaning an indirect reference; Illusion is a noun meaning a false impression or appearance.[^8][^12]

- **Allusion**: Indirect reference.
- His comment was an allusion to Shakespeare.
- **Illusion**: A false impression.
- The magician’s trick was just an illusion.


## Already vs. All Ready

Already is an adverb meaning by now; all ready is a phrase meaning completely prepared.[^12]

- **Already**: By now.
- She had already left when he arrived.
- **All ready**: Completely prepared.
- They were all ready for the trip.


## Altogether vs. All Together

Altogether is an adverb meaning entirely; All together means everyone/everything in one place.[^12]

- **Altogether**: Completely.
- Altogether, the team agreed to try again.
- **All together**: Everyone united.
- We were all together at dinner.


## Altar vs. Alter

Altar is a noun meaning a sacred platform; Alter is a verb meaning to change.[^12]

- **Altar**: A sacred platform.
- They married at the altar.
- **Alter**: To change.
- Please alter the plans as needed.


## Complement vs. Compliment

Complement is something that completes or enhances; Compliment is praise or a flattering remark.[^12]

- **Complement**: Something that completes.
- The wine complemented the meal.
- **Compliment**: Praise.
- She gave me a compliment about my coat.


## Conscience vs. Conscious

Conscience is a noun for one’s sense of right and wrong; conscious is an adjective meaning awake or aware.[^15][^8][^12]

- **Conscience**: Sense of morality.
- Her conscience told her to apologize.
- **Conscious**: Awake or aware.
- He was still conscious after surgery.


## Continuous vs. Continual

Continuous means without interruption; continual means recurring frequently, possibly with pauses.[^12]

- **Continuous**: Unbroken.
- The humming was continuous.
- **Continual**: Recurring.
- The phone rang at continual intervals.


## Counsel vs. Council

Counsel is advice or one who advises; council is a governing group.[^12]

- **Counsel**: Advice or advisor.
- He asked for legal counsel.
- **Council**: Governing group.
- The council meets monthly.


## Desert vs. Dessert

Desert is a dry, sandy area; dessert is the sweet course at a meal.[^12]

- **Desert**: Dry area.
- Camels live in the desert.
- **Dessert**: Sweet food.
- We had cheesecake for dessert.


## Device vs. Devise

Device is a noun for a tool or instrument; devise is a verb meaning to invent or plan.[^12]

- **Device**: A tool or gadget.
- The medical device was approved.
- **Devise**: To invent.
- She will devise a new strategy.


## Discreet vs. Discrete

Discreet is an adjective for careful or prudent; discrete means separate or distinct.[^12]

- **Discreet**: Careful.
- They were discreet in asking questions.
- **Discrete**: Separate.
- The experiment had three discrete phases.


## Elicit vs. Illicit

Elicit is a verb meaning to draw out; illicit is an adjective meaning illegal or forbidden.[^12]

- **Elicit**: To draw out.
- The comedian’s joke elicited laughter.
- **Illicit**: Illegal.
- They arrested him for illicit activity.


## Emigrate vs. Immigrate vs. Migrate

Emigrate means to leave one’s country; Immigrate means to enter another country; Migrate means to move (within a country or seasonally, often used for animals).[^12]

- **Emigrate**: Leave a country.
- They emigrated from Ireland.
- **Immigrate**: Arrive in a country.
- He immigrated to Canada last year.
- **Migrate**: Move within an area.
- Birds migrate every spring.


## Eminent vs. Imminent

Eminent means distinguished; imminent means about to happen.[^12]

- **Eminent**: Distinguished.
- The eminent professor won the award.
- **Imminent**: About to happen.
- A storm is imminent.


## Everyday vs. Every Day

Everyday is an adjective meaning ordinary; every day is an adverb phrase meaning each day.[^12]

- **Everyday**: Ordinary.
- He wore his everyday shoes.
- **Every day**: Each day.
- She exercises every day.


## Fewer vs. Less; Amount vs. Number; Many vs. Much

Fewer, number, many are for countable nouns; less, amount, much are for uncountable nouns.[^12]

- **Fewer**: Smaller count.
- I have fewer apples than you.
- **Less**: Smaller mass/quantity.
- There is less water in the pool.


## Hear vs. Here

Hear is a verb for perceiving sound; here is an adverb meaning in this location.[^12]

- **Hear**: To perceive sound.
- Did you hear the music?
- **Here**: In this place.
- Please sit here.


## Its vs. It’s

Its is possessive for “it;” it’s is the contraction for “it is” or “it has”.[^12]

- **Its**: Belonging to it.
- The dog wagged its tail.
- **It’s**: It is/has.
- It’s raining outside.


## Lay vs. Lie

Lay is a verb meaning to put or place (takes an object); Lie is a verb meaning to recline or rest (no object).[^8][^12]

- **Lay**: To put or place.
- Lay the keys on the table.
- **Lie**: To recline.
- I need to lie down.


## Lead vs. Led

Lead is a verb/to guide or a noun/a metal; led is the past tense of lead.[^15][^12]

- **Lead**: To guide or metal.
- He will lead the team; the pipe is made of lead.
- **Led**: Guided in the past.
- She led the group yesterday.


## Loose vs. Lose

Loose is an adjective meaning not tight; lose is a verb meaning to misplace or not win.[^15][^8]

- **Loose**: Not tight.
- The shoes are loose.
- **Lose**: Misplace or fail to win.
- Do not lose your keys.


## Passed vs. Past

Passed is the past tense of pass; past means earlier in time or beyond a point.[^15]

- **Passed**: Went by or succeeded.
- He passed the exam.
- **Past**: Previous time.
- In the past, things were different.


## Principal vs. Principle

Principal can be a noun meaning the head of a school or sum of money, or an adjective meaning foremost; Principle is a noun meaning a fundamental law or belief.[^15][^12]

- **Principal**: School leader or most important.
- The principal welcomed us.
- **Principle**: Law or belief.
- She strives to live by principle.


## Raise vs. Rise

Raise is a verb meaning to lift (takes an object); rise is an intransitive verb meaning to go up.[^15]

- **Raise**: To lift.
- Please raise your hand.
- **Rise**: To go up.
- The sun rises at dawn.


## Stationary vs. Stationery

Stationary means not moving; stationery means writing materials.[^12]

- **Stationary**: Not moving.
- The car was stationary.
- **Stationery**: Writing paper.
- He bought new stationery.


## Their vs. There vs. They’re

Their is the possessive of they; there refers to location; they’re is the contraction for “they are”.[^12]

- **Their**: Belonging to them.
- Their cat is friendly.
- **There**: Place or location.
- The cat is over there.
- **They’re**: They are.
- They’re going to the movies.


## To vs. Too

To is a preposition or infinitive marker; too is an adverb meaning also or excessively.[^12]

- **To**: Direction or infinitive.
- We went to the park.
- **Too**: Also or very.
- I want cake too.


## Your vs. You’re

Your is the possessive of “you;” you’re is the contraction for “you are”.[^12]

- **Your**: Belonging to you.
- Is this your book?
- **You’re**: You are.
- You’re invited.
